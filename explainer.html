WHAT IS A PERPETUAL FUTURES CONTRACT?

A perpetual futures contract lets you bet on the price of an asset — say BTC — without ever owning it. Unlike traditional futures (which expire on a set date), perpetuals never expire. You can hold them indefinitely.

But if perpetuals never expire, how does the price stay close to spot? That's where the funding rate comes in.

// analogy
Think of it like renting a position instead of buying an asset.
Every 8 hours, one side pays the other a "rent" to keep the market balanced.
// section 02

WHAT IS A FUNDING RATE?

Every 8 hours, exchanges run a settlement. If the perpetual price is trading above spot, longs pay shorts (positive rate). If it's trading below spot, shorts pay longs (negative rate). This nudges the perpetual price back toward the underlying asset.

// real example
BTC on Binance — funding rate: −0.05% every 8h

This means: shorts are paying longs 0.05% of position size every 8 hours.
On a $10,000 long position: you receive $5.00 every 8h → $15/day → $450/month.

Rates are usually tiny — often ±0.01% to ±0.1% per period — but they compound. And when rates get extreme (>0.2%/period), that's where arb opportunities emerge.

// section 03

WHY DO RATES DIFFER BETWEEN EXCHANGES?

Each exchange has its own user base, liquidity depth, and market sentiment. The funding rate is determined by that exchange's order book — not a global consensus. So the same asset on two different exchanges can have radically different funding rates.

1
Liquidity Imbalance
A smaller exchange with fewer traders may see more concentrated positioning — one large player going long can push the rate very negative.
2
User Base & Sentiment
Retail-heavy exchanges tend to be more long-biased (everyone wants to "buy the dip"). More longs = negative funding = shorts get paid.
3
Hedging Demand
Institutional players hedge on specific exchanges, creating predictable rate patterns. Miners often short BTC perps to lock in prices — pushing those exchange rates negative.

HOW THE ARB WORKS — REAL EXAMPLE

The goal: collect the spread between two exchanges while staying delta-neutral (immune to price moves). Here's a concrete $1,000 trade.

// setup
Exchange A (Bybit): BTC funding = −1.00% / 8h → longs RECEIVE
Exchange B (OKX): BTC funding = +0.50% / 8h → shorts receive
Capital deployed: $500 per leg ($1,000 total)
1
Open LONG on Exchange A (Bybit)
$500 long BTC. Because rate is −1%, you are paid 1% every 8 hours: +$5.00 / 8h
2
Open SHORT on Exchange B (OKX)
$500 short BTC. Because rate is +0.50%, shorts receive: +$2.50 / 8h
3
Wait for funding settlement
Every 8 hours, both payments clear. The long and short positions cancel each other on price movement — if BTC drops $100, the long loses $X but the short gains $X. Net = $0 on price.
4
Collect the spread
$5.00 + $2.50 = $7.50 per 8 hours. Three periods/day = $22.50/day on $1,000.

// delta-neutral explained
You are simultaneously LONG on one exchange and SHORT on another for the same asset.
BTC goes up $1,000? Long gains $500, short loses $500. Net = $0.
BTC drops $1,000? Long loses $500, short gains $500. Net = $0.

Price direction doesn't matter. Only the funding spread matters.
// section 05

THE RISKS

Funding arb is not free money. Here's what can go wrong:

📉
Spread Compression
Funding rates can reverse or converge quickly. A 1% spread can collapse to 0.05% in a few hours, eliminating the edge. You exit having collected less than expected.
⚡
Execution Slippage
Opening/closing two legs simultaneously is hard. Price can move between your two fills, creating unintended directional exposure — especially on low-liquidity pairs.
🏦
Exchange Counterparty Risk
Your funds sit on centralized exchanges. Exchange hacks, insolvency, or withdrawal restrictions are real risks (see: FTX). Diversifying across exchanges helps but doesn't eliminate it.
📊
Rate Inversion
If the funding rate flips after you enter (the exchange you're long on now has positive funding), you start paying instead of receiving. Monitoring is critical.
// margin & liquidation risk
Even with delta-neutral positioning, extreme short-term price moves can trigger margin calls on one leg before the other catches up. Always maintain sufficient buffer capital on both sides.

WHY PAPER TRADE FIRST?

Paper trading means simulating every trade with real-time market data but no actual capital at risk. Before deploying real money:

1
Validate the signal
Does the scanner actually find real opportunities, or are the rates stale by the time the trade executes? Paper trading reveals the true fill quality.
2
Measure true edge
Gross spread ≠ net profit. Subtract maker/taker fees (often 0.02–0.05% per fill × 4 fills = 0.08–0.20%) and see what's left. Many opportunities vanish after fees.
3
Tune the exit logic
When does the system close? At spread compression below 0.1%? After N periods? Paper trading lets you iterate without losing real capital.
4
Build statistical confidence
100+ paper trades gives you a win rate, average P&L, max drawdown — real numbers to justify capital allocation when you go live.
